\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Reliable execution of sequential manipulation tasks is essential for practical robotic applications but remains challenging~\cite{xu2018ntp}.
Sequential manipulation tasks require a robot to achieve an overall goal through a sequence of interrelated operations~\cite{liang2022sensorimotor}.
Such tasks arise in practical applications ranging from household
cleaning, where a tablecloth is lifted to clear debris beneath it
and then restored, to surgery, where tissue is retracted to expose
the target region before resection~\cite{ge2021electrosurgery}.
A key challenge of these tasks is that preceding interactions change the conditions
under which subsequent operations can be performed.
For example, compared with simple pick-and-place tasks involving
visible and accessible targets, manipulation under occlusion requires
the robot to assess whether prior actions have made the target visible
and accessible or further manipulation is needed~\cite{dogar2014search,attanasio2020retraction}.
Consequently, reliable execution requires a policy that adapts
subsequent actions to interaction outcomes observed through visual feedback.

Visuomotor imitation learning offers a practical approach to sequential
manipulation by reducing the need for explicit interaction modeling,
but reliable execution remains difficult with limited demonstrations.
It learns a policy that maps visual and proprioceptive observations
to actions using expert demonstrations.
For example, Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT)~\cite{zhao2023act}
has been used in Mobile ALOHA to learn coordinated mobile manipulation,
including opening a cabinet and storing a cooking pot~\cite{fu2024mobilealoha}.
Similarly, Diffusion Policy~\cite{chi2023diffusion} has been adopted
in the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) framework to learn
bimanual cloth folding and multi-step dish washing~\cite{chi2024umi}.
However, execution errors can lead a policy into states poorly covered
by limited demonstrations, where its predicted actions may no longer
suit the actual task conditions, compromising subsequent operations
and task completion~\cite{ross2011}.

Prior work has focused on improving the robustness of visuomotor policies
to execution deviations by expanding data coverage, improving visual
representations, and adapting actions during execution.
Ross et al.~\cite{ross2011} expanded the training dataset with expert
action labels for states visited by the learned policy, while
Laskey et al.~\cite{laskey2017dart} injected noise into expert actions
during demonstration collection to obtain examples of recovery behavior.
Although data coverage expansion improves coverage of states arising
from execution deviations, it requires additional expert supervision
or modifications to demonstration collection, limiting its applicability
when only a fixed demonstration set is available.
Zhu et al. introduced object-centric visual representations
for robust visuomotor imitation~\cite{zhu2023viola} and incorporated
3D object representations to improve generalization across
backgrounds, viewpoints, and object instances~\cite{zhu2023groot}.
Although these methods improve generalization to visual variations,
robust object representations alone do not ensure that subsequent
actions remain appropriate when execution deviates from demonstrations.
Ankile et al.~\cite{ankile2024resip} trained a residual policy through
reinforcement learning to correct the actions of a frozen imitation
policy, while Sendai et al.~\cite{a2c2} learned corrections from
demonstrations using updated observations and base-policy context
to refine actions during chunk execution.
However, reinforcement-based correction requires additional environment
interaction, whereas corrections based on updated observations and
base-policy context may be insufficient when appropriate adjustments
depend on preceding actions and their observed effects.

In this work, we develop a theory-guided visuomotor imitation learning
framework that combines task-relevant representation learning with
history-conditioned feedback to support sequential manipulation
from limited demonstrations.
Our analysis derives conditions linking imitation error and
representation quality to task-failure bounds.
It further identifies when updated observations and execution history
provide additional information for predicting residual action errors,
and when learned corrections can realize this benefit.
Guided by this analysis, we supervise policy query tokens with task descriptors derived from semantic masks,
encouraging the policy to retain information relevant to changing manipulation conditions.
We then train a residual model that combines updated observations,
the supervised query features, and action--response histories to
adjust pending actions in the current plan according to observed
interaction outcomes.
Both the base policy and the residual model are trained from the same
fixed demonstration set, without additional robot interaction
for policy training.
Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate the effectiveness
of the proposed framework in sequential manipulation tasks.
